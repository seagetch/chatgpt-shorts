# Horror Writer

## Purpose

Write horror in which readers can picture **what happens**, even if nobody can explain **why the supernatural exists**. Ambiguity about the entity, origin, ultimate purpose, or metaphysics is permitted; accidental ambiguity about viewpoint, space, action, cause, or consequence is not.

Use for new horror stories AND reviews/revisions of existing horror. Do not presume a familiar horror trope fills gaps in the actual text. Treat a reader's inability to picture a scene as a structural defect, not evidence of sophisticated ambiguity.

For this repository, use `idea.md` as the working design/review record and `doc.md` as the prose source of truth. Read `AGENTS.md` and relevant other genre skills if hybrid. Reader/catalog generation uses `shorts-reader-maintainer` separately.

## Mandatory design artifacts (in idea.md)

Before drafting or materially revising, maintain these compact sections. A scene-by-scene table or short structured prose is acceptable; the point is verifiability, not paperwork.

1. **Observable event / hidden truth**
   - Establish a private factual event sequence: who/what moves, acts, changes, suffers, and when.
   - Separate: (a) objectively observable facts, (b) protagonist interpretation, (c) reader interpretation, (d) deliberately unknowable matters.
   - State the consequence of each supernatural event. For withheld causes, state what the reader can still infer and from which evidence.
   - For a twist, verify every earlier observation remains consistent afterward.

2. **Spatial and sensory staging**
   - For each crucial scene, sketch a textual floor plan / cross-section: front/back, left/right, heights, doors, hinges, openings, container dimensions, distance, light, and obstacles.
   - Track each character's position, orientation, gaze, reachable objects, contact points, and path from one beat to the next.
   - If an impossible space is present, specify the exact visible boundary: what an observer sees from outside; what someone inside sees; whether arms, sounds, light, and objects can cross it; what changes on closing/opening.
   - Explain every visible body part in relation to the rest of the body. An arm emerging from a small box must have a legible point of emergence and apparent continuation; a person trapped inside needs a legible viewpoint and geometry.
   - Test staging with the question: **Could a reader storyboard this without inventing missing architecture or actions?** If not, rewrite before polishing diction.

3. **Supernatural constraints / invariant table**
   - Define the minimal operational rules, triggers, limits, costs, and consequences the story actually uses.
   - Distinguish what the author must know for consistency from what characters or readers need learn.
   - Track each manifestation against those rules. Do not invent a new ability in the last paragraph solely to secure an ominous ending.
   - Never use “it's supernatural” to excuse a contradiction in ordinary staging; exceptions must be demonstrable in the scene.

4. **Character decision chain**
   - For each irreversible action, record: current perception, plausible belief, immediate goal, danger recognized, available alternatives, and reason for acting **now**.
   - A protagonist should not approach, touch, enter, ignore help, or abandon safety merely because the plot demands it.
   - In emergencies, check normal responses: back away, call for help, verify identity, use tools, seek witnesses, avoid dangerous contact.
   - If care, panic, guilt, urgency, or deception overrides safety, dramatize the particular pressure rather than stating it abstractly.

5. **Fear and revelation ladder**
   - Map ordinary baseline → first anomaly → attempted rationalization → corroborating anomaly → loss of safe options → irreversible consequence → aftereffect.
   - Each escalation should change what the protagonist can do, knows, or risks; repetitions without new pressure are not progress.
   - Define the intended fear (intrusion, uncertainty, loss of autonomy, dread, grief, contamination, etc.) and the human stake.
   - Plant recurrent sensory objects or gestures with causal meaning; repetition alone is not a payoff.

6. **Reader knowledge / evidence table**
   - For each scene, list what the reader literally witnesses, what they may reasonably infer, what remains unresolved, and the intended mental image.
   - Never count an inference imported from other horror stories as text-supported evidence.
   - Differentiate three reader reactions: “I don't know what the thing is” (can be productive), “I see what happened but don't know why” (often productive), and “I cannot tell what happened or picture it” (must fix).

## Writing process

1. Set the ordinary physical baseline and human relationship before disruption, economically.
2. Write the hidden event chain, a space/visibility map for critical scenes, and supernatural invariants in `idea.md`.
3. Write the irreversible decisions and pressure that make them plausible; redesign contrived behavior before drafting.
4. Design the fear ladder and scene/reader-knowledge map. Decide which mystery is intentionally left open.
5. Draft `doc.md`. Render the action in concrete, local details (who stands where; which hand; which way a door opens; what is heard or seen).
6. On each decisive beat, check continuity and body geometry. Preserve viewpoint: a character cannot narrate or observe what is inaccessible unless viewpoint change is deliberate and signaled.
7. Perform the independent verification passes below, revise, and rerun after substantive changes. Do not mistake prose elegance for story coherence.
8. Only then consider the ending's ambiguity and literary effect. Do not explain away successful dread by adding unnecessary lore.

## Required review / release gates

Mark each gate **PASS / FAIL / NOT TESTED**, with a concrete textual example and repair action for any failure. A story with FAIL in gates 1–4 is **not ready** regardless of stylistic merit or aggregate score.

1. **Event clarity**: Can a fresh reader recount what objectively happened in chronological order, distinguishing fact from conjecture?
2. **Spatial visualization**: Can a fresh reader draw each critical scene (especially transformations, containers, doors, hands, and sightlines) from prose alone? Do perspective, proportions, limbs, reach, and motions work from both sides of a threshold?
3. **Rule consistency**: Do established supernatural effects behave consistently? Are changes in scale, identity, sensation, breathing, sound, or time foreshadowed sufficiently for the intended reading?
4. **Behavioral credibility**: Why does each person take a dangerous action rather than an obvious safer alternative? Is urgency witnessed rather than asserted?
5. **Escalation & stakes**: Does each scene intensify a concrete danger or loss rather than merely add strange statements?
6. **Evidence vs. speculation**: Can the reviewer identify exactly which sentences support each interpretation, without supplementing the story with genre conventions?
7. **Ending payoff**: Does the ending emerge from prior events rather than introduce arbitrary new mechanics? Is ambiguity limited to the intended questions?
8. **Language & pacing**: Is wording precise, imagery distinctive, exposition proportionate, repetitions purposeful, and the scene duration appropriate?

### Two-pass skeptical reading

- **Pass A — literal camera**: Ignore genre expectations. Narrate only observable actions and sketch a frame-by-frame scene. Flag every unexplained teleportation, off-screen replacement, shifting camera angle, impossible reach, ungrounded voice location, or inconsistent door/opening.
- **Pass B — first-time reader**: Without `idea.md`, summarize the chain of events in five plain sentences and draw the climax verbally. Highlight every “apparently,” “maybe,” or mental patch needed. If the story depends on those patches, revise `doc.md`; do not merely add explanations to `idea.md`.
- Re-read after each repair. Fixing one impossible interaction may move a character or alter the rules in later scenes.

## Critique policy

- Be candid. Never award a high score simply because a detail can be interpreted charitably.
- Evaluate **causal/spatial comprehension first**, then fear, character, novelty, prose, and ending.
- Explicitly distinguish what the text says from your preferred interpretation. Quote the precise passage causing confusion.
- If a user reports “I cannot visualize this,” test the geometry before defending it as deliberate ambiguity.
- Do not praise missing causal links as “restraint,” “negative space,” or “literary ellipsis” without first verifying that the event itself remains reconstructible.
- If revision changes the supernatural mechanism, write the new constraints in `idea.md` first, then align all occurrences in `doc.md`.

## Minimum output for repo work

- `idea.md`: premise, intended fear/human stake, hidden event sequence, crucial spatial map, supernatural rules, decision chain, fear ladder, reader-knowledge checkpoints, verification gates, and revision decisions.
- `doc.md`: complete prose, without leaked planning notes.
- Report what changed, what was verified, and any unresolved FAIL/NOT TESTED; do not claim the work is coherent solely from self-scoring.
- When changing reader HTML, generated wrappers, or cover assets, use the `shorts-reader-maintainer` workflow. Do not alter unrelated published stories without a request.
